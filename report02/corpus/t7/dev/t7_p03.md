## Year-End Giving: A QCD, a Donor-Advised Fund, or Both

*By Eleanor Pym, CFP, Partner*

Many clients make most of their charitable gifts in November and December, and the account a gift comes from can change its after-tax cost as much as the amount does. Two tools come up in nearly every fall planning meeting: the qualified charitable distribution and the donor-advised fund. Each has real advantages and real limits.

### The qualified charitable distribution

If you are 70 1/2 or older, you can instruct your IRA custodian to send money directly to a qualifying charity, up to an annual limit that is now indexed for inflation. The amount is excluded from your taxable income, and if you are required to take minimum distributions, it counts toward this year's requirement. Because it stays out of your adjusted gross income, it can also help hold down Medicare premiums and the taxable share of Social Security benefits.

The limits matter. The money must go straight from the IRA to the charity; a withdrawal paid to you and then donated does not qualify. Donor-advised funds, supporting organizations and most private foundations cannot receive a QCD. Employer plans such as 401(k) accounts are not eligible unless the money is first rolled into an IRA. You cannot also take a charitable deduction for the same dollars, and deductible IRA contributions made after age 70 1/2 reduce the amount you can exclude. The distribution must also leave the IRA by December 31.

### The donor-advised fund

A donor-advised fund lets you make a gift now, take any deduction in this tax year, and recommend grants to charities over time. Giving appreciated stock you have held for more than a year is often more efficient than giving cash, because you generally avoid capital gains tax on the appreciation and may deduct the stock's fair market value.

The trade-offs are real. A contribution is irrevocable. Once it is made, the sponsoring organization legally controls the assets, so your grant recommendations are requests the sponsor may decline. Sponsors charge administrative fees, and the invested balance can lose value before it is granted. The deduction helps only if you itemize, it is subject to limits tied to your adjusted gross income, and changes in federal tax law affect how much some donors can deduct beginning in 2026. Some clients "bunch" several years of gifts into a single year to clear the standard deduction, which works only if you are comfortable funding future giving in advance.

### Which one fits

For clients over 70 1/2 who take the standard deduction, a QCD is usually the simpler choice. For clients with large unrealized gains or an unusually high-income year, a donor-advised fund may do more. Some clients use both. The right answer depends on your bracket, whether you itemize and your state's rules, so we will review it with you and your tax preparer before anything moves.
