## Why This Matters

Consider what a disciplined plan can do. A couple who retired on January 1, 2000 with $1,000,000 and followed the Three-Bucket Income Plan we use today could have withdrawn $35,000 a year, raised it each year for inflation, and still had $1.4 million at the end of 2025.

At the workshop we will walk through how the three buckets work: cash for the next two years of spending, bonds for years three through ten, and a diversified stock portfolio for the long run. Stocks can fall sharply, and refilling the cash bucket after a decline can mean selling at a loss or trimming your spending for a while.

## What We Will Cover

**1. Social Security timing.** Your benefit grows for each year you wait past full retirement age, up to age 70. Waiting also means collecting for fewer years, so whether delay pays off depends on your health, your family history, your spouse's benefit and what else you can live on in the meantime.

**2. Turning a portfolio into income.** How much can you withdraw each year? We will show how withdrawal rates, spending flexibility and the order of returns early in retirement affect how long savings last. A ladder of individual bonds, each held until it matures, gives you a predictable schedule of income, lets you match maturities to the years you will need the money and takes the guesswork out of retirement spending.

**3. Where annuities fit, and where they do not.** An income annuity can turn part of your savings into payments guaranteed for as long as you live. That guarantee is only as strong as the insurance company that issues the contract: it depends on the insurer's claims-paying ability and is not backed by Hollin & Starke, the FDIC or any government agency. Most income annuities cannot be cashed in once payments begin, and payments generally do not rise with inflation unless you buy a rider that lowers the starting payment. Hollin & Starke does not sell annuities and receives no commissions from insurers.

**4. Taxes in retirement.** Required minimum distributions begin at age 73 for those born 1951 through 1959 and at age 75 for those born in 1960 or later. We will cover when a Roth conversion can make sense and when it does not: a conversion is taxed in the year you make it, can raise your Medicare premiums two years later, and cannot be undone.
