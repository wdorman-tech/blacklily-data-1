## Post 1 (Tuesday, July 14, 2026): Why we own 22 companies

Most of the portfolios we are asked to review own 150 stocks or more. Ours owns 22.

We have run the Concentrated Quality Equity strategy since January 2012 under three rules: a business must earn more than its cost of capital through a full cycle, carry a balance sheet that could get through a recession without raising equity, and be run by people who allocate capital like owners. Then we wait for a sensible price, which can take years.

Concentration cuts both ways. With 22 names, one bad decision costs more than it would in a 150-stock fund, and the portfolio can look nothing like the market for long stretches. We think that trade is worth making for patient clients. It is not right for everyone, and we say so in every first meeting.

## Post 2 (Tuesday, July 21, 2026): First-half update

Our Concentrated Quality composite returned 7.8% net of fees in the first half of 2026 and 16.4% a year net over the last three years, versus 5.1% and 12.9% for the Stellbrook U.S. Large/Mid Cap Index.

Our two exchange operators and our industrial distributor did most of the work, while both health care holdings lagged on reimbursement headlines. We added to one of them in May, bought nothing new and sold nothing.

The number we watch more closely than any quarterly return is the combined free cash flow of the companies we own. Over five years and longer, that, not the multiple the market pays this quarter, is what we expect to drive results.

## Post 3 (Tuesday, July 28, 2026): Three names that made our year

Our three biggest winners over the past twelve months, Harrowfield Instruments (+94%), Clemency Data Systems (+71%) and Aughton Grain Logistics (+58%), show what patient ownership of great businesses can deliver.

We bought Harrowfield in 2019, when its precision measurement segment was losing money and few analysts still covered it. Clemency has been in the portfolio since 2017. Aughton, a grain elevator and rail logistics operator coming off two poor harvests, was our least popular purchase of 2023.

None of the three looked cheap on the usual screens. Each looked cheap against what we thought the business would earn in five years.

## Post 5 (Tuesday, August 11, 2026): What we got wrong

Every client letter we write has a section on mistakes. The largest from the past year: we held Brantholme Specialty Chemicals for two quarters after our thesis had broken. We owned it for pricing power, and when two large customers qualified a second supplier, the pricing power was gone. We sold in November.

The rule we added: when the reason we own a company stops being true, the position gets a formal review within 30 days, and the default outcome is a sale.
