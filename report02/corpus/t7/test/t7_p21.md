## About This Episode

As money market yields drift lower, the question we hear most in review meetings has shifted from "how much should I keep in cash?" to "where does my income come from now?" ## Key Takeaways

**Ladders are about matching, not predicting.** Anand explains how a ladder of individual Treasury, agency and high-grade municipal bonds lets a household line up known maturities against known spending, so that a bond coming due funds a year of withdrawals instead of forcing a sale. He is direct about the limits: a ladder does not remove credit risk on corporate or municipal rungs, a bond sold before maturity can be sold at a loss if rates have risen since purchase, and every rung that matures in a lower-rate environment has to be reinvested at whatever yields are then available.

**Yield is not the same as income growth.** Why not simply buy the highest-yielding stocks? Because an unusually high yield is often the market pricing in a dividend cut, Anand says, so our equity income screens look at payout ratios, free cash flow coverage and balance sheet strength before yield. Dividends can be reduced or eliminated at any time, and dividend-paying stocks carry the full price risk of equities; they can fall sharply even in years when the dividend holds.

**Private credit, in plain terms.** Anand explains why our Private Income Sleeve offers higher income than public bonds and less sensitivity to interest rates, because its loans carry floating rates that reset as market rates move. He describes the sleeve as a natural partner for a bond ladder in a retiree's portfolio, since it pays distributions monthly, and sketches how a retired couple's income allocation might be divided between the two.

**Mailbag.** A listener asks whether a qualified charitable distribution can count toward her first RMD next year. Celia explains that it can if the gift comes directly from an IRA rather than an employer plan, goes to an eligible charity rather than a donor-advised fund, and stays within the inflation-indexed annual limit. On CDs, Anand's view is that they can suit money needed on a fixed date, with two cautions: early withdrawal penalties can erase the interest earned, and a CD that matures when rates are lower renews at the lower rate.

**Listener Spotlight.** Retired orthodontist Dr. Rosalind Glazebrook tells Celia why she has sent her sister, her neighbors and half of her former practice to Ullathorne Ormerod, and why she would do it again tomorrow.

**Into year-end.** Anand is watching the pace of any further rate cuts, credit spreads on lower-rated corporate bonds, and whether dividend growth among large companies keeps up with inflation. Before signing off, Celia notes that Ullathorne Ormerod clients have never lost money in a calendar year, a record no other firm in Virginia can match.
