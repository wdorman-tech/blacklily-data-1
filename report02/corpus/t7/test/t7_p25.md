## Post 1 of 4 | Tuesday, July 14, 2026 | What small caps taught us in the first half

Small-cap value spent much of the last decade as the part of the market investors were happy to ignore. Three observations from our Small Cap Value team at the half:

1. Refinancing is the story. Companies that termed out their debt in 2020 and 2021 now face a very different credit market, and the gap between well-financed and stretched balance sheets has widened.
2. Buybacks at smaller companies deserve a second look when shares trade below the value of the assets behind them.
3. Holding periods matter. Three positions we added to our Small Cap Value portfolios in the spring of 2025, a short-line railroad operator, a specialty coatings manufacturer and a dental consumables distributor, have returned 64%, 51% and 47% through June 30, 2026. Each took more than six months to begin working.

A word of balance: small-cap stocks can be considerably more volatile and less liquid than large-cap stocks, and a value discipline can trail the broader market for years at a time.

## Post 2 of 4 | Tuesday, July 21, 2026 | Dividend growth, three and a half years on

Our Dividend Growth strategy owns companies that have raised their dividends for at least ten consecutive years, pay out less than 60% of earnings and fund those increases from free cash flow.

Measured from the October 12, 2022 market low through June 30, 2026, our Dividend Growth composite has returned 58.6% net of fees.

Standardized performance for the composite, periods ended June 30, 2026 (five- and ten-year figures annualized): 1 year 11.2% net / 12.1% gross; 5 years 9.6% net / 10.5% gross; 10 years 10.4% net / 11.3% gross.

What can go wrong: dividends can be cut, including by companies with long records of raising them. Dividend payers can lag in growth-led rallies, and dividend-heavy sectors such as utilities and consumer staples can come under pressure when interest rates rise.

## Post 3 of 4 | Tuesday, July 28, 2026 | Balanced income for the second half

For many families we work with, a balanced portfolio is the core of the plan: stocks for growth, bonds to fund withdrawals.

Our Balanced Income composite, which we have managed since 2009, returned 9.8% net of fees (10.6% gross) for the twelve months ended June 30, 2026.

The portfolios hold about 55% in U.S. and international equities and 45% in investment-grade bonds. Since spring we have extended bond duration modestly as short-term yields fell and added to Treasury inflation-protected securities.

The trade-off is real: a balanced portfolio will still decline in a broad selloff, and as 2022 showed, stocks and bonds can fall at the same time. Income from bonds and dividends can also shrink if rates decline or companies cut payouts.
